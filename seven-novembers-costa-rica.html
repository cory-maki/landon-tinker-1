Landon Tinker’s Seven Novembers Building Homes in Costa Rica

📅 September 16, 2026
✎ Landon Tinker
⌚ 3 min read

For seven years running, Landon Dean Tinker has spent each November in Costa Rica, building homes with his family through YWAM’s Homes of Hope program. For a Texas construction professional early in his career, those trips have become a second classroom, one where the lessons are about people as much as lumber.

The trips started as a family commitment and have stayed that way. Landon and his family return every year, which means the experience isn’t a one-off service project squeezed into a vacation. It takes planning months ahead, time away from school and work, and a willingness to trade comfort for long days of physical labor. That consistency is the point. Showing up once is generous; showing up seven years in a row is a commitment.

What a Volunteer Build Looks Like

Homes of Hope builds are fast, focused, and hands-on. Volunteers work in teams to frame walls, lift them into place, and close in a structure that a family will move into. The work is physically demanding, and it rarely goes exactly to plan. Lumber needs to be re-cut, a wall needs another set of hands, a crew needs to regroup after the heat of the afternoon.

As Landon sees it, the right mindset starts with humility. Volunteers aren’t there to impress anyone, and most tasks are small: holding a stud square, passing materials, cleaning up the site so the next step can happen. But every one of those tasks contributes to a family’s safety and stability. That perspective, that small jobs matter because the whole depends on them, is one he carries into his professional work with builders in Texas.

Showing up once is generous. Showing up every November for seven years is a commitment.

Lessons That Transfer to the Job Site

There’s a surprising amount of overlap between a volunteer build in Costa Rica and a professional construction site. Both depend on sequencing: you can’t set a roof before the walls are square. Both depend on clear communication across a team with mixed experience levels. And both punish shortcuts. A wall that’s rushed today becomes someone else’s problem tomorrow.

Tinker saw the same principles at work during his internships with Shellback Construction and David Weekley Homes. The scale and the tools were different, but the fundamentals weren’t. A good job site runs on preparation, respect for the people doing the work, and attention to detail. His path from early jobs into construction shows how those habits formed even before his first internship.

Building Relationships, Not Just Houses

Because Landon returns every year, the trips have become a continuation of relationships rather than a series of isolated efforts. That continuity gives the work a depth that a single visit can’t, and it has given him a lasting appreciation for the communities he serves and the cultures he gets to know.

It has also shaped how he thinks about the people on the other end of the work. A finished house isn’t the goal in itself; the family who moves in is. That idea sits at the center of his thinking on homebuilding that starts with people, and it explains why the Costa Rica trips remain a fixed point on the family calendar. Conclusion

Seven Novembers in, Landon’s commitment hasn’t faded. It has become part of who he is and how he works, on volunteer builds and professional job sites alike. For a fuller picture, there’s more on Landon’s experience in homebuilding and service.
